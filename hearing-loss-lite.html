Hearing Loss Simulator

Set up your sound first. Your computer's settings change what you hear.

  • Set the volume. Set your computer's volume so it sounds like someone talking to you from across a table (about 60 dB). Then leave the volume alone. The sample skips the faders, so you can replay it at any time to reset your volume.
  • The video's own volume. Turn the volume slider inside the video player all the way up, and make sure it is not muted. This goes for a YouTube video too: check the speaker button in the YouTube player before you connect it.
  • Headphones. Use headphones if you want to experience different hearing levels in your left and right ears.
Tell me more, and troubleshooting sound on your computer
  • Which volume number? There is no one right number, because every computer and pair of headphones is different. For most it is near the middle of the volume slider. Go by how loud the sample conversation sounds, not by the number.
  • No sound at all? Check that the computer is not muted, that the volume is up, and that your headphones are plugged in or connected. If you right-clicked this tab and chose "Mute site", un-mute it.
  • Changing the volume later. Turning the computer up partway through cancels out the hearing loss you are simulating. That is what a hearing aid does.
  • Speakers. Laptop speakers are weak at very low and very high pitches, and both ears hear both sides. Headphones give the truest result.
  • Bluetooth. Bluetooth headphones add a delay and often cannot play the highest pitches. Wired headphones are better.
  • Sound effects. Turn off bass boost, equalizers, "loudness" or "enhance audio" settings, and spatial audio. They change which pitches are loud.
  • Mono audio and balance. For separate ear settings, turn off "mono audio" in the computer's accessibility settings, and keep the left and right balance in the middle.
  • A teacher who wants to be exact: with speakers, a sound level meter app held where the listener sits should read about 60 dB while the sample conversation plays.

    Script: the guided tests

    These are six quick tests designed to show which speech sounds get harder to hear when pitches are missing or changed. Each test is a short example built around one group of speech sounds. For each one, I'll say what to focus on, play it, then tell you what it means.

    One. Everyday speech. Listen.

    This morning the weather was warm, so we walked to the park and sat on a bench.

    Two. Low, humming sounds, like m, n, and oo. Listen.

    My uncle hums low and warm along the road, morning and noon.

    If that was hard to hear, the trouble is in the low pitches.

    Three. Middle sounds, like k, g, ch, and rr. Listen.

    Chuck gave Rachel a large cherry cake.

    If that was hard to hear, the trouble is in the middle pitches.

    Four. High, hissing sounds, like s, f, and th. Listen.

    Sue sits by the sea, sifting through thistles.

    If that was hard to hear, the trouble is in the high pitches.

    Five. Crispness. Sharp sounds, like p, t, and k, mark where words start and stop. Listen.

    Pick up the cup. Tap the top. Kick the bucket back.

    If the words ran together, you're missing the middle and high pitches.

    Six. Look-alike words. Each pair differs by one soft, high sound, like th and f. Listen.

    Thin, fin. Sip, ship. Sick, thick.

    If the pairs sounded the same, the trouble is in the high pitches.

    Mystery hearing loss: the faders are hidden

    Sound picture. Each bar is one pitch, lined up with the chart above. Gray is the sound being played. Pink is what gets through the hearing loss. Where you see gray with no pink, that part of the sound is lost.

    How loud is the sound in real life? Set this with the green Sound level tag on the left edge of the chart. The sound you play stands in for a real-life sound that loud. Slide the tag down to hear how much of a louder sound, such as shouting, still gets through. It never plays louder than the original.
    More true to life
    What do these do? Background noise adds the steady hum of a busy room to whatever is playing. Ringing is heard on top of everything; the faders cannot remove it. Unclear speech smears neighboring pitches together, the way a damaged inner ear does, so speech stays muddy even when it is loud enough. Hearing aid adds back about half of the loss at each pitch, a long-used rule of thumb for fitting one. Its result is drawn on the chart as a dashed line marked A. It makes sounds louder but does not clear up unclear speech.

    How to use the simulator

    1. Start a sound. At the top, choose a file, YouTube, or the microphone, and get it playing.
    2. Choose a hearing loss. Click a degree, a shape, or one of each. Clicking a shape shows its typical form and lights the degree it usually comes with. Click a different degree and the shape keeps its outline but slides up or down the chart to that level. Click a lit shape to turn it off. Click a lit degree to go back to Normal.
    3. Fine-tune with the faders. Drag a fader down for more loss at that pitch. The line across the middle of the fader marks its exact value, and a label shows the pitch and the number while you point at it or move it. For small steps, click a fader and press the arrow keys: 5 dB a press, or 1 dB with Shift held down.
    4. Try louder sounds. The green Sound level tag on the left edge of the chart, with its green line across the chart, is not a hearing-loss fader. It sets how loud the sound you are playing is meant to be in real life. Drag it down the dB scale to hear what still gets through when the same sound is a shout or a siren. Wherever a fader sits below the green line, that pitch cannot be heard.
    5. Make the ears different. Choose Separate ears next to "Faders set", or click a "Different in each ear" button. Each pitch then has a red O for the right ear and a blue X for the left ear, and each one moves only its own ear. Separate ear settings are meant to be used with headphones; through speakers, both ears hear both sides.
    6. Set the volume first. Follow the yellow box at the top of the page: play the sample conversation and set the volume so it sounds like people talking across a table.
    7. Test your own high-pitch hearing. On the right side of the chart, the row of buttons marked 9K to 20K each play a pure tone at that pitch (9K means 9,000 Hz) for a few seconds. The first time, a sound check asks whether you can hear a plain beep, to make sure the computer's sound is on. The tones do not go through the faders. Whether you hear one depends on your ears and also on your speakers or headphones: many laptop speakers and Bluetooth headphones cannot play the highest pitches. Keep the volume where it is; turning it up to hear a tone can hurt your ears even when the tone seems silent.

    What this cannot show

    The faders only make sounds quieter. Real hearing loss often also makes speech unclear even when it is loud enough. The "Unclear speech" setting above the chart gives a rough idea of that. One thing the simulator leaves out: with a real hearing loss, very loud sounds can feel just as loud as they do to anyone else.

    Parts of the ear and types of hearing loss

    The type of hearing loss depends on which part of the ear is not working.

    See the picture: Parts of the ear

    Outer ear

    Pinna and ear canal. They collect sound and carry it to the eardrum.

    Conductive hearing loss

    Sound is blocked on the way in. Causes include earwax, an ear canal infection, an object in the canal, or an ear canal that did not form fully.

    Middle ear

    Eardrum, three tiny bones (malleus, incus, stapes), the air space behind the eardrum, and the Eustachian tube. They pass the sound's vibrations on to the inner ear.

    Conductive hearing loss

    The vibrations do not get through well. Causes include fluid from an ear infection, a hole in the eardrum, or bones that are stiff or damaged.

    Inner ear and hearing nerve

    Cochlea, balance canals, and the nerve to the brain. The cochlea turns vibrations into nerve signals.

    Sensorineural hearing loss

    The cochlea or the nerve is damaged. Causes include aging, loud noise, genetics, some illnesses and some medicines. It is usually permanent.

    Mixed hearing loss is a conductive loss and a sensorineural loss in the same ear.

    Conductive loss mostly makes sounds quieter, which is what this simulator does. Sensorineural loss also makes sounds less clear. Turn on "Unclear speech" above the chart for a rough idea of that.

    Words to know

    Audiogram
    The chart. A hearing test marks the quietest sound a person can hear at each pitch. The lower the marks, the greater the hearing loss. Any sound pictured above a person's line is too quiet for them to hear.
    Frequency (Hz)
    Pitch, measured in hertz. Low numbers are low pitches, like a hum. High numbers are high pitches, like birdsong or the sound "s". Each fader is one frequency.
    Decibels (dB HL)
    Loudness on the hearing-test scale. 0 dB HL is the quietest sound a typical young ear can hear. A fader at 60 means a sound at that pitch has to reach 60 dB before the person hears it.
    Degree
    How much hearing loss: Normal (0 to 20 dB), Mild (21 to 40), Moderate (41 to 70), Severe (71 to 90), Profound (91 to 120). When the loss is different at different pitches, the degree is named from the pure-tone average: the average of the losses at 500, 1000 and 2000 Hz.
    Shape
    How the loss changes from low pitches to high pitches. Audiologists call this the configuration. A "high-frequency loss" slopes down to the right. A "cookie bite" dips in the middle.
    Speech banana
    The yellow area on the chart. It shows where the sounds of speech fall when someone talks at a normal level.
    Right and left ears
    On an audiogram the right ear is drawn in red with circles and the left ear in blue with X's.
